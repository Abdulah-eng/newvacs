ASSESSMENT
Persistent Nonvalvular Atrial Fibrillation – Elevated Stroke Risk with Self-Adjusted Anticoagulation Due to Fear of Bleeding
Angela Brooks is a 64-year-old female with persistent nonvalvular atrial fibrillation and elevated thromboembolic risk who presents after independently reducing anticoagulation exposure because of bruising concerns. The patient reports skipping multiple doses of apixaban over the past several weeks after noticing minor bruising and becoming concerned about potential bleeding complications.
Importantly, the patient's bruising appears most consistent with minor anticoagulation-associated bruising rather than clinically significant bleeding. She denies epistaxis, gastrointestinal bleeding, hematuria, hospitalization, emergency department visits, or any other evidence of major bleeding. Hemoglobin remains stable at 13.0 g/dL compared with 13.2 g/dL previously, platelet count is normal, and no objective evidence suggests a major bleeding event.
The primary medication therapy problem is not excessive anticoagulation but rather fear-driven nonadherence. The patient demonstrates incomplete understanding of expected anticoagulation effects and has overestimated the clinical significance of minor bruising. She also appears heavily influenced by anecdotal information from a friend's experience rather than individualized risk assessment.
Given her elevated CHA₂DS₂-VASc score and ongoing indication for stroke prevention, continuation of full-dose apixaban remains appropriate. Current information does not support dose reduction or discontinuation of anticoagulation. The priority should be patient education, reassurance, and shared decision-making to improve adherence while helping the patient recognize warning signs that would warrant medical evaluation.
Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF) – Clinically Stable
The patient reports stable heart failure symptoms without evidence of recent decompensation. Mild fatigue and exertional dyspnea remain unchanged. She denies edema, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, hospitalization, or emergency department visits.
Improved medication affordability has positively affected adherence. At this time there is no evidence suggesting worsening heart failure or need for urgent therapy modification. Continued monitoring and reinforcement of adherence remain appropriate.
Health Literacy Deficit – Improving but Continues to Affect Medication Decisions
The patient continues to demonstrate gaps in understanding regarding atrial fibrillation, stroke prevention, and anticoagulation management. Although she now understands that apixaban helps prevent stroke, she remains uncertain about the balance between stroke prevention and bleeding risk.
These knowledge deficits directly contributed to self-adjustment of therapy. Additional education using patient-centered language and teach-back methods is warranted to improve confidence and long-term adherence.
