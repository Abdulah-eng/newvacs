Why These Guidelines Matter
Major depressive disorder is one of the most common behavioral health conditions encountered in ambulatory care practice and is a leading cause of disability worldwide.
Depression affects:
• Quality of life
• Daily functioning
• Employment
• Relationships
• Chronic disease management
• Medication adherence
• Healthcare utilization
• Mortality
Depression frequently coexists with anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and tobacco use.
Pharmacists play an important role in:
• Screening and identification
• Medication selection
• Monitoring treatment response
• Assessing adherence
• Managing adverse effects
• Identifying treatment-resistant depression
• Facilitating behavioral health referrals
• Supporting long-term maintenance therapy
The ACP guideline focuses primarily on acute treatment selection, while the VA/DoD guideline provides additional guidance regarding measurement-based care, treatment-resistant depression, suicide risk assessment, and long-term management.
Diagnostic Criteria for Major Depressive Disorder
A diagnosis of major depressive disorder requires:
Five or more symptoms present during the same two-week period
At least one symptom must be:
• Depressed mood
OR
• Loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia)
Additional symptoms may include:
• Sleep disturbance
• Fatigue
• Appetite changes
• Weight changes
• Impaired concentration
• Psychomotor agitation or retardation
• Feelings of guilt or worthlessness
• Recurrent thoughts of death or suicide
Symptoms must cause clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or functional status.
Screening and Assessment
PHQ-9
The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is the preferred screening and monitoring tool in ambulatory care.
PHQ-9 Severity Interpretation
Minimal Depression
PHQ-9 Score: 0-4 
Mild Depression
PHQ-9 Score: 5-9 
Moderate Depression
PHQ-9 Score: 10-14 
Moderately Severe Depression
PHQ-9 Score: 15-19 
Severe Depression
PHQ-9 Score: 20-27 
Clinical Pearl
A PHQ-9 score of 10 or greater generally warrants further assessment and consideration of treatment.
The VA/DoD guideline strongly supports measurement-based care using validated symptom scales throughout treatment.
Treatment Goals
The goal of depression treatment is not simply symptom improvement.
The goal is remission.
Response
Response is generally defined as:
≥50% reduction in symptom severity
Patients may still experience residual symptoms despite response.
Response is encouraging but should not be considered the final treatment goal.
Remission
Remission refers to:
Resolution or near-resolution of depressive symptoms
Patients who achieve remission experience:
• Improved functioning
• Better quality of life
• Reduced relapse risk
• Improved long-term outcomes
Remission should be the primary treatment target.
Recovery
Recovery occurs when remission is sustained over time.
Patients transition from acute treatment to maintenance therapy.
Initial Treatment Selection
ACP Guideline Recommendations
Mild Depression
ACP suggests:
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) Monotherapy
as an initial treatment option for many patients with mild depression.
Consider pharmacotherapy when:
• CBT is unavailable
• Symptoms worsen
• Prior depressive episodes exist
• Patient preference favors medication
Moderate-to-Severe Depression
ACP recommends:
CBT
OR
A Second-Generation Antidepressant (SGA)
as first-line treatment.
ACP also suggests that:
CBT + Antidepressant Combination Therapy
may be considered in appropriate patients.
Treatment decisions should be individualized using shared decision-making.
Antidepressant Selection
No single antidepressant is universally preferred.
Selection should be based on:
• Symptom profile
• Comorbid conditions
• Adverse effect concerns
• Tobacco use
• Weight concerns
• Sleep disturbances
• Cost
• Patient preference
Sertraline
Advantages
• Strong depression efficacy
• Strong anxiety efficacy
• Extensive clinical experience
• Generally well tolerated
Potential Concerns
• Gastrointestinal effects
• Sexual dysfunction
Escitalopram
Advantages
• Excellent anxiety efficacy
• Simple dosing
• Well tolerated
Potential Concerns
• Sexual dysfunction
• QT prolongation at higher doses
Bupropion
Advantages
• Weight neutral
• Lower risk of sexual dysfunction
• Useful for tobacco cessation
Potential Concerns
• May worsen anxiety
• Avoid in seizure disorders
• Avoid in eating disorders
Mirtazapine
Advantages
• Useful for insomnia
• Useful for poor appetite
• Lower sexual dysfunction risk
Potential Concerns
• Sedation
• Weight gain
SNRIs
Examples
• Duloxetine
• Venlafaxine
Advantages
• Depression and anxiety efficacy
• May benefit patients with chronic pain
Potential Concerns
• Increased blood pressure
• Nausea
• Withdrawal symptoms if abruptly discontinued
Suicide Risk Assessment
The VA/DoD guideline emphasizes routine suicide risk assessment.
Evaluate for:
• Suicidal thoughts
• Suicidal intent
• Access to lethal means
• Previous suicide attempts
• Recent psychiatric hospitalization
Patients with active suicidal ideation require urgent intervention and escalation of care.
Monitoring After Initiation
Follow-up should occur within:
4-8 weeks
Assess:
• PHQ-9 score
• Medication adherence
• Adverse effects
• Sleep
• Appetite
• Energy
• Functional status
• Suicidal ideation
• Patient goals
Common Adverse Effects
SSRIs
• Nausea
• Diarrhea
• Headache
• Sexual dysfunction
• Insomnia
SNRIs
• Nausea
• Sweating
• Elevated blood pressure
• Sexual dysfunction
Bupropion
• Insomnia
• Anxiety
• Tremor
• Dry mouth
Mirtazapine
• Sedation
• Increased appetite
• Weight gain
Inadequate Response
Before declaring treatment failure, confirm:
✓ Correct diagnosis
✓ Adequate medication dose
✓ Adequate duration of therapy
✓ Medication adherence
✓ Presence of psychosocial stressors
✓ Presence of comorbid anxiety
✓ Presence of substance use
Many patients appear to have treatment failure when the primary issue is nonadherence or inadequate treatment duration.
Treatment-Resistant Depression (TRD)
Treatment-resistant depression generally refers to:
Failure of at least two adequate antidepressant trials
Patients with TRD often experience:
• Greater functional impairment
• Higher relapse rates
• Increased healthcare utilization
• Greater suicide risk
Management options include:
• Switching antidepressants
• Augmentation strategies
• Combination therapy
• Cognitive behavioral therapy
• Psychiatric referral
• Esketamine treatment
• Other advanced therapies
The ESCAPE-TRD Trial discussed in Week 5 examines escalation strategies for patients with treatment-resistant depression.
Duration of Therapy
First Depressive Episode
Continue treatment for:
At least 6-12 months after remission
to reduce relapse risk.
Recurrent Depression
Patients with:
• Multiple depressive episodes
• Severe depression
• Chronic depression
may require long-term or indefinite treatment.
Role of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)
CBT remains one of the most effective nonpharmacologic treatments for depression.
Benefits include:
• Symptom improvement
• Relapse prevention
• No medication adverse effects
• Improved coping skills
• Long-term benefits beyond acute treatment
CBT may be used:
• Alone
• In combination with antidepressants
• Following medication failure
• During maintenance treatment
