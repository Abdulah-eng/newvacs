Why These Guidelines Matter
Tobacco use remains the leading preventable cause of disease, disability, and death in the United States.
Smoking contributes to:
• Cardiovascular disease
• Stroke
• Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
• Multiple cancers
• Chronic kidney disease
• Diabetes complications
• Poor mental health outcomes
• Increased mortality
Many patients with depression and anxiety use tobacco at higher rates than the general population.
Behavioral health conditions and tobacco use frequently reinforce one another, making cessation particularly challenging.
Pharmacists play a critical role in:
• Identifying tobacco use
• Assessing readiness to quit
• Recommending pharmacotherapy
• Providing behavioral counseling
• Managing withdrawal symptoms
• Preventing relapse
• Supporting long-term abstinence
Tobacco Use Disorder
Nicotine is highly addictive.
Repeated nicotine exposure leads to:
• Physical dependence
• Psychological dependence
• Withdrawal symptoms upon cessation
Common withdrawal symptoms include:
• Irritability
• Anxiety
• Restlessness
• Difficulty concentrating
• Increased appetite
• Cravings
• Sleep disturbances
Many patients mistakenly believe smoking improves anxiety or stress.
In reality, nicotine withdrawal often contributes to the anxiety symptoms that improve temporarily after smoking.
The 5 A's Framework
The USPSTF and VA/DoD guidelines support routine tobacco assessment using the 5 A's.
Ask
Identify tobacco use at every encounter.
Examples:
• Cigarettes
• Cigars
• Smokeless tobacco
• Electronic nicotine products
Advise
Provide clear and personalized advice to quit.
Example:
"Quitting smoking is one of the most important things you can do to improve your health."
Assess
Determine readiness to quit.
Questions may include:
• Have you considered quitting?
• What concerns do you have about quitting?
• Have you tried to quit before?
Assist
Provide:
• Pharmacotherapy
• Counseling
• Educational resources
• Quit plans
Arrange
Schedule follow-up.
Ongoing support significantly improves quit success.
Readiness to Quit
Patients generally fall into one of three categories:
Not Ready to Quit
Focus on:
• Education
• Motivational interviewing
• Addressing barriers
Ambivalent
Focus on:
• Exploring motivations
• Discussing benefits
• Identifying concerns
Ready to Quit
Focus on:
• Selecting pharmacotherapy
• Setting a quit date
• Creating a cessation plan
First-Line Pharmacotherapy
VA/DoD Preferred Options
Varenicline
Combination Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT)
These are generally considered the most effective first-line options.
Varenicline
Mechanism
Partial agonist at the α4β2 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor.
Benefits:
• Reduces cravings
• Reduces withdrawal symptoms
• Reduces smoking satisfaction
Dosing
Days 1-3
• 0.5 mg once daily
Days 4-7
• 0.5 mg twice daily
Day 8 and beyond
• 1 mg twice daily
Typical duration:
• 12 weeks
Longer treatment may be appropriate for some patients.
Common Adverse Effects
• Nausea
• Vivid dreams
• Insomnia
• Headache
Clinical Pearl
The VA/DoD guideline supports varenicline as one of the most effective pharmacologic options for tobacco cessation.
Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT)
NRT works by reducing withdrawal symptoms while eliminating exposure to combustible tobacco products.
Long-Acting NRT
Nicotine Patch
Provides continuous nicotine delivery.
Examples:
• 21 mg daily
• 14 mg daily
• 7 mg daily
Short-Acting NRT
Examples:
• Nicotine gum
• Nicotine lozenge
• Nicotine inhaler
• Nicotine nasal spray
Used for breakthrough cravings.
Combination NRT
Combination therapy generally includes:
Nicotine Patch
PLUS
Gum or Lozenge
Benefits:
• Improved craving control
• Improved quit rates
• Recommended by VA/DoD for many patients
Bupropion SR
Mechanism
Norepinephrine and dopamine reuptake inhibitor.
Benefits:
• Reduces nicotine cravings
• Reduces withdrawal symptoms
• Treats depression
Dosing
Days 1-3
• 150 mg once daily
Day 4 and beyond
• 150 mg twice daily
Begin treatment approximately one week before the quit date.
Advantages
• Depression treatment
• Lower risk of weight gain
• May be useful in patients with depression and tobacco use disorder
Potential Concerns
• Insomnia
• Anxiety
• Dry mouth
• Seizure risk
Clinical Pearl
Because Week 5 focuses on depression and anxiety, bupropion creates an important clinical decision point.
Bupropion may be attractive for patients with:
• Depression
• Tobacco use disorder
• Sexual dysfunction concerns
However, it may not be the best initial option when significant anxiety symptoms are present.
Behavioral Counseling
Both USPSTF and VA/DoD emphasize that counseling should accompany pharmacotherapy whenever possible.
Examples include:
• Individual counseling
• Group counseling
• Telephone quit lines
• Cognitive behavioral therapy
• Motivational interviewing
Combining counseling with pharmacotherapy improves quit rates compared with either intervention alone.
Tobacco Cessation in Patients with Depression and Anxiety
This is one of the most important concepts for Week 5.
Historically, clinicians worried that quitting smoking could worsen depression or anxiety.
Current evidence suggests:
• Tobacco cessation improves long-term mental health outcomes
• Depression should not prevent quit attempts
• Anxiety should not prevent quit attempts
• Behavioral health patients benefit from cessation interventions
Patients should be monitored closely during quit attempts for:
• Mood changes
• Anxiety changes
• Withdrawal symptoms
• Relapse risk
Relapse Prevention
Relapse is common.
Many successful quitters require multiple quit attempts before achieving long-term abstinence.
When relapse occurs:
DO NOT label the attempt as a failure.
Instead:
• Identify triggers
• Reassess motivation
• Adjust pharmacotherapy
• Reinforce future quit attempts
Monitoring
At follow-up visits assess:
• Tobacco use status
• Cravings
• Withdrawal symptoms
• Medication adherence
• Adverse effects
• Mood symptoms
• Anxiety symptoms
• Relapse triggers
