Heart Failure Guideline Summary
Based on:
2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure
Why Heart Failure Matters
Heart failure affects millions of patients in the United States and is a leading cause of hospitalization, morbidity, mortality, and healthcare expenditures.
Heart failure is not a single disease but a clinical syndrome resulting from structural or functional cardiac abnormalities that impair ventricular filling or ejection of blood.
Patients with heart failure commonly experience:
Dyspnea
Fatigue
Exercise intolerance
Fluid retention
Recurrent hospitalizations
Reduced quality of life
Ambulatory care pharmacists play a critical role in optimizing guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), improving medication adherence, monitoring for adverse effects, and reducing the risk of hospitalization and death.
The primary goal of heart failure management is not only symptom improvement but also prevention of disease progression, hospitalization, and mortality.
Heart Failure Classification
Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF)
Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤40%
This population has the strongest evidence supporting guideline-directed medical therapy.
Most mortality-reducing therapies were studied in patients with HFrEF.
Heart Failure with Mildly Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFmrEF)
LVEF 41–49%
Patients may benefit from therapies similar to HFrEF, although evidence is less robust.
Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction (HFpEF)
LVEF ≥50%
Management focuses on symptom reduction, prevention of hospitalization, management of comorbid conditions, and risk-factor modification.
Therapeutic options have historically been limited but have expanded significantly with the introduction of SGLT2 inhibitors.
Heart Failure Staging
ACC/AHA Stage A
At risk for heart failure but without structural heart disease or symptoms.
Examples:
Hypertension
Diabetes
Obesity
Coronary artery disease
Goal:
Prevent development of symptomatic heart failure.
ACC/AHA Stage B
Structural heart disease without signs or symptoms of heart failure.
Examples:
Prior myocardial infarction
Reduced ejection fraction without symptoms
Goal:
Prevent progression to symptomatic heart failure.
ACC/AHA Stage C
Structural heart disease with current or prior symptoms of heart failure.
Most ambulatory heart failure patients fall into this category.
ACC/AHA Stage D
Advanced heart failure requiring specialized interventions.
Examples:
Refractory symptoms
Frequent hospitalizations
Consideration of advanced therapies
NYHA Functional Classification
Class I
No limitation of physical activity.
Class II
Slight limitation of physical activity.
Comfortable at rest.
Ordinary activity causes symptoms.
Class III
Marked limitation of physical activity.
Less-than-ordinary activity causes symptoms.
Class IV
Symptoms at rest.
Unable to perform physical activity without discomfort.
Foundational Therapy for HFrEF
Modern HFrEF management centers on four foundational medication classes.
These therapies should generally be initiated as early as possible and titrated to maximally tolerated doses.
1. ARNI
Sacubitril/Valsartan (Entresto)
Preferred renin-angiotensin system inhibitor for most patients with symptomatic HFrEF.
Benefits:
Reduces mortality
Reduces hospitalization
Improves symptoms
Monitoring:
Blood pressure
Renal function
Potassium
Common barriers:
Cost
Hypotension
2. Evidence-Based Beta Blockers
Only three beta blockers have demonstrated mortality benefit in HFrEF:
Metoprolol succinate
Carvedilol
Bisoprolol
Benefits:
Reduced mortality
Reduced hospitalization
Improved ventricular remodeling
Monitoring:
Heart rate
Blood pressure
Symptoms of worsening heart failure
Common barriers:
Fatigue
Bradycardia
Patient concern regarding symptom worsening
3. Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists (MRAs)
Examples:
Spironolactone
Eplerenone
Benefits:
Reduced mortality
Reduced hospitalization
Monitoring:
Potassium
Serum creatinine
eGFR
Common barriers:
Hyperkalemia
Gynecomastia (spironolactone)
4. SGLT2 Inhibitors
Examples:
Dapagliflozin
Empagliflozin
Benefits:
Reduced hospitalization
Reduced cardiovascular mortality
Renal protection
Importantly, benefits occur regardless of diabetes status.
Monitoring:
Renal function
Volume status
Genitourinary adverse effects
Common barriers:
Cost
Insurance coverage
Practical GDMT Sequencing
Historically, medications were added sequentially over many months.
Current practice emphasizes rapid initiation of all four foundational therapies whenever feasible.
A practical ambulatory care approach:
Initiate beta blocker and ARNI (or ARB/ACEI if appropriate).
Add SGLT2 inhibitor early.
Add MRA when renal function and potassium allow.
Titrate therapies every 2–4 weeks as tolerated.
The goal is rapid implementation of life-saving therapies rather than waiting to achieve target doses before adding additional agents.
Management of HFpEF
Evidence-based options are more limited than for HFrEF.
Important management strategies include:
SGLT2 inhibitors
Blood pressure control
Diuretic therapy for congestion
Management of atrial fibrillation
Weight management
Treatment of comorbid conditions
Unlike HFrEF, the primary goals are symptom improvement and reduction in hospitalization risk.
Monitoring Parameters
Pharmacists should routinely monitor:
Clinical Monitoring
Weight
Dyspnea
Edema
Exercise tolerance
Blood pressure
Heart rate
Laboratory Monitoring
Serum creatinine
eGFR
Potassium
Sodium
Monitoring should occur after initiation and titration of heart failure medications.
Common Medication Therapy Problems
Examples frequently encountered in ambulatory care include:
Failure to initiate all four foundational therapies
Subtherapeutic dosing
Lack of medication titration
Cost-related nonadherence
Hyperkalemia concerns preventing MRA use
Failure to monitor renal function
Inadequate patient education
Ambulatory Care Clinical Pearls
Every patient with HFrEF should be evaluated for all four foundational medication classes.
Start therapies early and titrate frequently.
Do not delay initiation of one therapy while waiting to optimize another.
SGLT2 inhibitors benefit patients regardless of diabetes status.
Blood pressure reductions are often acceptable if the patient remains asymptomatic.
Medication adherence often has a greater impact on outcomes than small differences in drug selection.
Monitoring and follow-up are essential components of successful heart failure management.
