VACS: Virtual Ambulatory Care Simulator
Clinical Case Development Project
By Premier Pharma Mentors
1. Project Purpose
Alyssa,
You are being asked to support the development of VACS: Virtual Ambulatory Care Simulator, Premier Pharma Mentors’ web-based pharmacy education platform.
The goal of VACS is to give pharmacy students a realistic, clinically rigorous environment where they can practice reviewing patient charts, interviewing AI patients, identifying medication-related problems, writing SOAP notes, and receiving structured preceptor feedback.
The end goal is to develop realistic patient cases based on the clinical guidelines for the top 10 ambulatory care disease states and the top 11 inpatient disease states in America. However, we will begin with one prototype week before expanding to the full case library.

2. Immediate Assignment
Your immediate assignment is to build the prototype content for:
Week 1: Hypertension + Type 2 Diabetes
This Week 1 prototype will become the model for future disease-state blocks across the VACS platform.
Do not build the full 5-week experience yet. Do not build all top 10 ambulatory or inpatient disease states yet. The priority is to make Week 1 excellent so that it can serve as the master template for future case development.

3. Overall VACS Student Experience
The student experience should be designed around the following workflow:
Student logs into VACS.
Student sees the assigned disease-state block.
Student opens the assigned patient chart.
Student reviews the EMR-style patient profile.
Student interviews the AI patient.
Student moves between the chart, patient interview, workup section, and SOAP note.
Student writes and submits a SOAP note.
AI grades the SOAP note.
Dr. Kurunwune, the virtual preceptor, provides feedback.
Student applies feedback to the next encounter.
The cases should feel clinically realistic. Students should need to gather information, interpret trends, identify missing data, assess safety, and make patient-specific recommendations.

5. Week 1 Structure
Week 1 should include:
Monday: Guideline Review Day
Students review the primary guidelines and key clinical decision points for hypertension and type 2 diabetes.
The Monday content should prepare students to manage patients during the rest of the week. Since this content is for pharmacy students, it should not over-explain basic terminology. Instead, it should focus on guideline-based clinical application.
Key areas to include:
Blood pressure classification and treatment thresholds
Blood pressure goals by patient type
First-line antihypertensive therapy
When to initiate monotherapy versus combination therapy
Lifestyle interventions with expected clinical impact
A1c goals and individualization of therapy
Type 2 diabetes medication selection
Metformin use and contraindications
GLP-1 receptor agonists
SGLT2 inhibitors
Kidney protection
ASCVD risk reduction
Statin use in patients with diabetes
Renal monitoring
UACR and eGFR interpretation
Medication safety monitoring
Counseling priorities
Follow-up timing
Common student mistakes
Tuesday: Initial Visits
Students see all three patients for the first time.
Tuesday should be foundational but not overly simple. The goal is for students to:
Review the chart
Identify uncontrolled hypertension and/or diabetes
Recognize missing labs or missing history
Assess current medications
Interview the patient effectively
Identify adherence, lifestyle, access, or monitoring gaps
Develop an initial care plan
Write a complete SOAP note
Wednesday: 3-Month Follow-Up
The same three patients return three months later.
Wednesday should be intermediate-level. The goal is for students to:
Reassess blood pressure response
Reassess A1c response
Interpret new labs
Review home blood pressure and/or glucose data
Assess adherence
Assess side effects
Determine whether the initial plan worked
Modify therapy based on patient-specific factors
Document a stronger SOAP note
Thursday: Second 3-Month Follow-Up
The same three patients return another three months later.
Thursday should be advanced-level. The goal is for students to:
Manage more complex follow-up decisions
Adjust therapy based on incomplete or evolving response
Address cost, access, adherence, side effects, or patient preference barriers
Recognize safety concerns
Apply guideline-based escalation
Counsel the patient clearly
Create an appropriate follow-up and monitoring plan
Friday: Journal Club / Reflection
Friday should reinforce clinical decision-making from the week.

Topic: SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists in patients with type 2 diabetes and cardiorenal disease**
The Friday assignment should require students to answer:
What did the article or evidence teach you?
How does this evidence apply to one of this week’s patients?
Which patient would benefit most from this therapy and why?
What patient-specific factors would affect the medication decision?
What monitoring, counseling, or access issues would need to be addressed?

12. Acceptable Alternative Answers
For each encounter, list clinically reasonable alternative recommendations.
This section should help the AI grader and Dr. Kurunwune distinguish between:
The preferred answer
Other acceptable clinical answers
Unacceptable or unsafe answers
Examples of acceptable alternatives may include:
ACE inhibitor versus ARB when clinically appropriate
Thiazide-like diuretic versus calcium channel blocker depending on patient factors
Optimizing metformin before adding another agent
Adding a GLP-1 receptor agonist for ASCVD/weight benefit
Adding an SGLT2 inhibitor for kidney or heart failure benefit
Deferring medication changes temporarily if adherence is the main issue
Ordering missing labs before making a medication change
Reassessing home blood pressure technique before intensifying therapy
Also list unacceptable answers.
Examples:
Intensifying therapy without assessing adherence
Starting or increasing medication without checking renal function when needed
Ignoring hypoglycemia symptoms
Missing albuminuria in a patient with diabetes
Failing to address cost barriers
Recommending a therapy contraindicated by patient-specific factors
Creating a plan with no follow-up or monitoring

14. Dr. Kurunwune Teaching Points

Each guide should include:
What the student should have done well
Common student mistakes
Teaching pearls
Follow-up questions Dr. Kurunwune should ask
How the student can improve before the next encounter
Example teaching questions:
“What additional information did you need before making your recommendation?”
“How did you determine whether this patient needed medication intensification versus adherence support?”
“Why does kidney function matter before recommending this medication?”
“Why does albuminuria change your treatment priorities in diabetes?”
“What would you monitor after starting this medication?”
“How would you explain this recommendation to the patient in plain language?”
“What would make this plan difficult for the patient to follow?”
These teaching points should help the student become a stronger clinical thinker, not just memorize the correct answer.
