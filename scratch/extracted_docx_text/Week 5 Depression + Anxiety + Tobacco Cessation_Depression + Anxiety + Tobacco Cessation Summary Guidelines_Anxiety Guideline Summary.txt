Why These Guidelines Matter
Anxiety disorders are among the most common mental health conditions encountered in ambulatory care practice.
Generalized anxiety disorder frequently coexists with:
• Major depressive disorder
• Tobacco use disorder
• Chronic pain
• Cardiovascular disease
• Substance use disorders
• Sleep disorders
Patients often present with both depression and anxiety symptoms simultaneously.
This is particularly important because:
• Anxiety can worsen depression outcomes
• Anxiety may affect antidepressant selection
• Anxiety can impair medication adherence
• Anxiety contributes to functional impairment and reduced quality of life
Pharmacists play an important role in:
• Screening
• Symptom assessment
• Medication selection
• Monitoring response
• Monitoring adverse effects
• Counseling
• Supporting behavioral interventions
• Tobacco cessation support
Diagnostic Criteria for Generalized Anxiety Disorder
Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) is characterized by:
Excessive anxiety and worry occurring more days than not for at least 6 months
Patients have difficulty controlling the worry.
Associated symptoms may include:
• Restlessness
• Feeling keyed up or on edge
• Fatigue
• Difficulty concentrating
• Irritability
• Muscle tension
• Sleep disturbance
Symptoms must cause clinically significant distress or impairment.
Screening and Assessment
GAD-7
The Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) questionnaire is the most commonly utilized screening and monitoring tool.
GAD-7 Severity Interpretation
Minimal Anxiety
GAD-7 Score: 0-4
Mild Anxiety
GAD-7 Score: 5-9
Moderate Anxiety
GAD-7 Score: 10-14
Severe Anxiety
GAD-7 Score: 15-21
Clinical Pearl
A GAD-7 score of 10 or greater generally warrants further evaluation and treatment consideration.
The USPSTF review found that validated screening tools such as the GAD-2 and GAD-7 demonstrate acceptable diagnostic accuracy for identifying anxiety disorders in adults.
Treatment Goals
The goal of anxiety treatment is not simply symptom reduction.
Goals include:
• Symptom control
• Improved daily functioning
• Improved quality of life
• Reduction in avoidance behaviors
• Improved sleep
• Improved social and occupational functioning
• Prevention of relapse
First-Line Treatment Options
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)
The VA/DoD guideline recommends CBT as a first-line treatment option for many patients with anxiety disorders.
Benefits include:
• Strong evidence base
• No medication adverse effects
• Long-term relapse prevention
• Improvement in coping skills
CBT may be used:
• Alone
• With pharmacotherapy
• Following medication failure
Pharmacologic Therapy
First-line medications include:
SSRIs
Examples:
• Sertraline
• Escitalopram
• Fluoxetine
• Paroxetine
SNRIs
Examples:
• Venlafaxine
• Duloxetine
These medications are effective for both anxiety and depression and are commonly selected when both conditions coexist.
Choosing an Antidepressant When Anxiety Is Present
Sertraline
Advantages
• Strong evidence for anxiety and depression
• Commonly used first-line
• Generally well tolerated
Potential Concerns
• GI adverse effects
• Sexual dysfunction
Escitalopram
Advantages
• Excellent anxiety efficacy
• Well tolerated
• Simple dosing
Potential Concerns
• Sexual dysfunction
Venlafaxine
Advantages
• Effective for anxiety and depression
• Useful when anxiety symptoms predominate
Potential Concerns
• Increased blood pressure
• Withdrawal symptoms if abruptly discontinued
Duloxetine
Advantages
• Effective for anxiety and depression
• May benefit patients with chronic pain
Potential Concerns
• Nausea
• Increased blood pressure
Bupropion
Clinical Pearl
Although bupropion is an effective antidepressant and tobacco cessation medication, it is generally not considered a preferred initial option when significant anxiety symptoms are present because anxiety may worsen in some patients.
This concept will be reinforced throughout the Week 5 patient cases.
Monitoring After Initiation
Follow-up should occur within:
4-8 weeks
Assess:
• GAD-7 score
• Medication adherence
• Adverse effects
• Sleep quality
• Functional status
• Stressors
• Depression symptoms
• Tobacco use
Common Adverse Effects
SSRIs
• Nausea
• Headache
• Diarrhea
• Sexual dysfunction
• Insomnia
SNRIs
• Nausea
• Sweating
• Elevated blood pressure
• Sexual dysfunction
Anxiety and Depression Frequently Coexist
One of the most important clinical pearls for ambulatory care providers is that anxiety and depression commonly occur together.
Patients with both conditions often experience:
• More severe symptoms
• Slower treatment response
• Greater functional impairment
• Higher relapse rates
• Reduced quality of life
Treatment plans should address both conditions simultaneously.
Anxiety and Tobacco Use
Many patients report using tobacco products to manage anxiety symptoms.
Although nicotine may provide temporary relief, long-term tobacco use often:
• Worsens anxiety symptoms
• Increases dependence
• Creates withdrawal-related anxiety
• Complicates behavioral health management
Pharmacists should assess:
• Smoking history
• Readiness to quit
• Previous quit attempts
• Relationship between anxiety symptoms and tobacco use
Inadequate Response
Before declaring treatment failure, confirm:
✓ Correct diagnosis
✓ Adequate dose
✓ Adequate duration
✓ Medication adherence
✓ Presence of psychosocial stressors
✓ Presence of comorbid depression
✓ Presence of substance use
Many patients require optimization before a medication is considered ineffective.
Duration of Therapy
Following symptom remission:
Continue treatment for at least 12 months
to reduce relapse risk.
Patients with recurrent or severe anxiety disorders may require longer-term treatment.
